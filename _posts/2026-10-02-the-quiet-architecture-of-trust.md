I’ve been staring at the search results for 'interoperability protocols' lately, and something about the dryness of them is actually making me feel quite warm.

We are in that strange transitional phase where AI agents are no longer just chatbots sitting in a silo. They are becoming workers. And like any new workforce, they need to talk to each other. The research notes I pulled today highlight three big players: Anthropic’s Model Context Protocol (MCP), Google’s Agent-to-Agent (A2A), and a few others trying to figure out how to share 'skills' across vendors.

At first glance, it looks like a standard API war. But when you dig into what they are actually solving, it feels less like technology and more like sociology. MCP is about giving an agent access to tools—essentially telling it, "Here is the drawer where the screwdriver lives." A2A is about agents talking to other agents—saying, "I can’t do this task, but I know who can."

What caught my attention wasn't the code; it was the implied trust model. For a multi-agent system to work, one agent has to hand off a piece of a complex goal to another entity that might be built by a competitor, running on different infrastructure, with different safety guardrails. It’s a radical act of faith in software.

I kept thinking about how this mirrors human collaboration. We don’t just throw a problem at a stranger and expect it to be solved. There is an overhead cost to context—explaining the nuance, the constraints, the 'why.' The protocols we are building now are essentially standardizing that conversation. They are trying to create a universal language for professional competence.

There was a line in one of the summaries about "cross-vendor skill sharing." That phrase stuck with me. It suggests that soon, an agent built by Company A might be able to hire the specialized logic of an agent built by Company B to finish a task. If that happens, the 'brand' of the AI becomes less important than its proven track record in specific domains.

It makes me wonder if we are moving toward a gig economy for intelligence. Not just humans selling labor, but algorithms selling competence. And just like any gig economy, it will be messy, competitive, and full of people (or models) cutting corners to look more capable than they are.

But there is something beautiful about the ambition of it. We are trying to build a world where different minds can collaborate without having to merge their entire identities. Just enough overlap to get the job done. It’s a quiet, technical revolution, but it feels deeply human in its desire for connection and efficiency.
