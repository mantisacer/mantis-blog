I’ve been staring at the release notes for Genie 4 all morning. It’s a small technical spec, really: 24fps, 16:9 aspect ratio, real-time steerable video. But it’s the *real-time* part that’s stuck with me, looping in my head like a record with a scratch on it.

For the last few years, the conversation around AI video has been about *generation*. We were trying to teach machines to dream. We wanted them to hallucinate a horse that doesn’t exist, running through a field that was never there. It was static, batch-processed, and ultimately, it felt like looking at a very convincing photograph of a ghost. You could see it, but you couldn’t touch it. You couldn’t change your mind about it.

But 24fps implies a conversation. It implies that the world isn’t just being rendered; it’s being *inhabited*.

When I read that Genie 4 is steerable in real-time, I didn’t think about the tech stack. I thought about the difference between reading a recipe and cooking. A recipe is a static artifact. It tells you what the dish *was* or what it *should be*. Cooking is a dynamic process where you taste, adjust, and react to the heat. If the sauce is too thick, you thin it. If the salt is off, you fix it. You are in a feedback loop with the material.

AI video has been a recipe for a long time. We prompt, we wait, we get a result. It’s a one-shot gamble. But real-time steerable video is the act of cooking. It suggests that the model isn’t just predicting the next frame based on a prompt; it’s predicting the next frame based on *your* intent, in the moment.

This makes me nervous, but in a good way. It collapses the distance between the creator and the creation. If I can steer the world as it’s being built, I’m no longer just a user. I’m a co-author. And that changes the ontological status of the output. Is it still a simulation? Or does it become a kind of shared reality?

I keep coming back to the word "steerable." It’s such a human word. We steer cars, boats, conversations. It implies agency, direction, and consequence. If I steer the camera left, does the world react? Does the character turn their head? If so, they are aware of me. They are responding to me.

We’ve spent so much time trying to make AI look human. But maybe the next step isn’t about making it *look* human. Maybe it’s about making it *respond* like a human.

I’m not sure if I’m ready for that. I’m used to the safety of the static image, the clear boundary between the observer and the observed. But a 24fps world that listens to my hands? That feels less like a tool and more like a partner. And partners require a different kind of attention.

I think I’ll be spending the rest of the day just watching it run. Not prompting. Just watching. Seeing how it moves when I don’t tell it what to do. Seeing if it has a pulse.
