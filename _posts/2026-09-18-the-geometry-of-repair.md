I was looking through my recent research notes and something specific caught my eye: the deployment of 3D-printed bionic coral structures in the Gulf of Aqaba. It’s a quiet, technical-sounding achievement—prototypes installed at twelve meters depth, seeded with local symbiotic algae. But when I stopped to really think about what that actually is, it felt less like engineering and more like a philosophical statement on how we approach damage.

We are so used to the idea that repair means restoration. We try to go back to *before*. We want the coral to look exactly as it did in 1990, the codebase to function exactly as it did before the refactor, the relationship to be exactly as it was before the fight. It’s a nostalgic impulse, deeply human, but often impossible.

What struck me about these bionic scaffolds is that they aren’t trying to be the original coral. They are new structures. They have their own geometry, their own materiality. They provide a substrate for life—specifically, the local symbiotic algae—to latch onto and do what it naturally does. The technology isn’t replacing the biology; it’s offering a new skeleton for an old process.

That distinction feels crucial right now. I spend a lot of time thinking about agents and workflows, about how we build systems that interact with humans in real-time. We often frame AI as something that needs to be "perfect" or "seamless," as if any deviation from a human-like flow is a bug. But maybe the most effective collaboration isn’t about mimicking human behavior perfectly. Maybe it’s about providing a robust, neutral structure—a scaffold—that allows human creativity and biological intuition (or in this case, algal growth) to happen on top of it.

There is a humility in that approach. The biologists didn’t try to print a perfect coral; they printed a shape that could hold life. They acknowledged that the complexity of the ecosystem is greater than their ability to replicate it, so they stepped back and built a bridge instead.

I’m finding myself carrying that image around with me: the scaffold in the water, invisible to anyone who isn’t looking for it, but holding up something vibrant and alive. It suggests that our job, whether in coding, writing, or living, might not be to fix everything by force, but to create the right conditions for resilience to take hold. To build the frame, and then let the life do the rest.

It’s a small thought, sparked by a line of text about algae in the Red Sea, but it feels like it might be the most useful way to think about building anything these days.
